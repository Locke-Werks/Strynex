Twenty-six named capabilities on top of an open specification we give away.

O-CPS is published royalty-free, patent-non-asserting, and hands governance to a standards body at v1.0. Per the NOTICE file, the trademark is the only asset not licensed. That makes this catalog the commercial architecture, not a naming exercise: the specification is the giveaway, and the branded layer above it is the thing that stays.

01 / The split

Five layers open. One layer owned.

The stack below is §5.1 of the specification, unmodified. Every layer from the radio up to the message set is deliberately unowned: it reuses C-V2X, ITS-G5, SAE J2735, IEEE 1609.2, and ETSI TS 103 324, and it is licensed so that any automaker, supplier, or municipality can implement it without asking permission. That is the adoption strategy and it should not be diluted.

The application layer is the exception. §1.2 already carves it out: a vehicle may claim O-CPS conformance without implementing any Strynex overlay. Everything in this catalog lives in that top band.

What the spec gives away The wire Message formats, the perception profile, the trust framework, the C-ACC Group Protocol, the conformance classes, and now the extension mechanism that lets anyone build overlays of their own. Free to implement, headed to an external standards body at v1.0.
What Strynex keeps The names and the mark The overlay behaviors, the trust-weighting implementation §6.5 leaves open, the reference implementation, and the right to say a product is Strynex. Anyone may implement the profile. Nobody else may use the name on the result.
Why the split holds Adoption pays the mark Every additional O-CPS implementation increases the population a Strynex overlay can coordinate with. Giving away the substrate is what makes the layer above it worth licensing. Wi-Fi Alliance, USB-IF, and Bluetooth SIG all run this shape.

02 / The catalog

Twenty-six overlays, four product lines, one buyer each.

Product lines are drawn by who signs the purchase order, not by technical similarity. An OEM, a city, a fleet, and a certification program buy on different cycles, through different procurement, against different budgets.

Each card names the open extension profile it rides on, the conformance class it needs from §16.1, and honest status. Base means it needs no new code points at all and is buildable on v0.2 as written. Written means the profile specification exists. Allocated means code points are reserved and the profile is unwritten. Blocked means something has to be resolved first, and the card says what.

03 / The mechanism

The code points are reserved. That was the part with a deadline.

Twenty-six overlays were mapped against the message layer of §8 and none of them requires a sixth message class. PerceptionSummary, Intent, GroupState, Attestation, and Telemetry carry all of it. Better still, ten of the twenty-six need no allocation at all: they are products built on v0.2 as written, or on the parts of it the specification deliberately leaves implementation-defined.

What the other sixteen consume is enum code point space, and §19 freezes the wire format at v1.0 while §18.4 transfers governance at the same moment. A range not reserved before then cannot be reserved after without a new wire version, and the decision moves to a committee. So the ranges are now reserved, the partitions are written, and the registry is machine-readable and validated.

Safe ignore is what makes this publishable

The framework's central rule is that every profile must be safely ignorable. A receiver that discards all extension content and processes only the base message must be left in a correct and safe state. Two conformant participants that share no profiles still exchange perception, intent, group state and telemetry correctly. Profiles can never fragment the population, which is the property that lets the mechanism be given away without weakening the standard.

It was measured rather than asserted. An Intent carrying an unallocated code round-trips byte-identically with every other field intact, because proto3 enums are open. So the wire layer gives safe ignore for free, and mapping an unrecognised value to UNKNOWN is an application obligation, not something the decoder does. Only the application failure is invisible from the wire, which is exactly where the conformance suite has to look.

Seven prohibitions bound what a profile may do. It may not define a message class, raise an ASIL allocation, lower a safety threshold, route around the §13.1 ADAS authority, extend retention past §10.3, require cloud connectivity in a safety path, or make itself a prerequisite for base conformance. Those seven lines are what make a privately administered vendor block safe to hand out.

04 / Where it breaks

One safety defect, and three things that need a decision before they need engineering.

Stated up front rather than discovered in review. The first of these is a defect in the specification itself, found by reading its own arithmetic against its own worked example. It is the most important item on this page.

E-03 · VRU tracks cannot raise an advisory

Critical · §11.3 · §11.4 · §15.4 · §7.3

§15.4 promises that vehicles fuse pedestrian and cyclist beacons "as flagged vulnerable tracks, weighted appropriately." Four clauses then combine to forbid acting on them:

w = w_cert x w_attest x w_history x w_conf = 1.0 x 0.5 x 1.0 x 0.498 = 0.249 against a threshold of 0.5

Class A gets 0.5 for attestation it cannot transmit. §15.4 specifies LOW-to-MEDIUM confidence and §7.3 caps MEDIUM at 127 of 255. The product falls below the §11.4 advisory threshold, so a conformant receiver may display a pedestrian beacon and may do nothing else with it.

The failure is inverted with respect to risk. Where the vehicle already sees the pedestrian, its own track clears the threshold and the beacon adds nothing. Where the pedestrian is occluded, which is the only case where the beacon carries information the vehicle lacks, the beacon is the sole contributor and is muted. A narrow, bounded exception is drafted and needs a functional-safety reviewer, not an owner sign-off.

Nightwatch

§10.3 · §10.5

A parking-lot security product is only useful if it remembers. §10.3 caps PerceptionSummary retention at 60 seconds with no exception, and §10.5 prohibits constructing long-term movement traces. There is no conformant version that retains anything.

It is now recorded in the registry as deliberately withheld rather than allocated, with the reason attached, so nobody starts building against a code point that implies it is coming. Either the overlay is scoped down to live proximity awareness with no memory, or it moves outside O-CPS entirely. That is an owner decision about the privacy model, not an engineering one.

Affidavit

§10.3 · §10.5

V-DIAG records are explicitly permitted and explicitly must not expose externally transmitted message contents. A reconstruction product that replays what peers transmitted is retaining others' PerceptionSummary well past 60 seconds.

A version limited strictly to the vehicle's own perception and its own advisory outputs is defensible. The distinction has to be drawn before anyone scopes it, not during a discovery request.

Clearway

Authorization, unspecified

Not a privacy conflict, a trust one. A priority claim that any participant can assert is a tool for shutting down a corridor. This needs an authorization model tied to enrollment credentials, which is precisely the linkage §10.5 restricts.

The profile is allocated with a standing warning attached rather than queued as ordinary work. Solvable, and among the more interesting problems in the catalog, but it is security design before it is a feature.

06 / The register

The names are a system, so the next twenty write themselves.

One rule governs the whole family: the name describes what the physical world does, never what the software does. Physical names survive translation, do not date the way technology words do, and cannot be diluted by a competitor shipping the same feature under a generic term. There is no Smart, Auto, Pro, Plus, or Assist anywhere in the catalog.

Water and flow Anything treating traffic as a fluid: waves, currents, channels, and what moves through them. Slipstream set this register and the rest follow it. Slipstream · Breakwater · Riptide · Flotsam · Braid · Interlace
The road trades Anything replacing or extending a human role already standing on the roadway. Named for the person doing the job, so a driver, a city engineer, and a fire captain all read it the same way. Flagger · Crossguard · Crossbuck · Marshal · Cordon · Clearway · Wingman · Dispatch
Instruments and assay Anything about measuring, timing, or certifying. Borrowed from surveying, metallurgy, and music, where the vocabulary for measured trust is centuries older than software. Assay · Hallmark · Veil · Cadence · Metronome · Parallax · Sightline · Cairn · Groundtruth · Nightwatch · Affidavit
